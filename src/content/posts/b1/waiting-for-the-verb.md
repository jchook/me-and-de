---
title: Where German Hides the Verb
date: 2026-07-26
level: B1
description: In a German subordinate clause, the verb arrives last, like the detective in the final chapter.
---

German has a habit of taking the action in a sentence, putting it at the far end, and asking everyone else to wait patiently until it is ready.

*Ich weiß, dass du den Kuchen, den meine Mutter gestern mit großer Liebe für die Party gebacken hat, ohne mich gegessen hast.*

The important part arrives at the end: *gegessen hast*. You ate the cake.

Before German gets around to admitting this, it gives you a cake, my mother, yesterday, great love, a party, and the fact that I was not invited. The sentence carries all this information along before revealing what happened to the cake.

This is verb-final order in a German subordinate clause. A clause beginning with *dass* puts its verb at the end:

*Ich weiß, dass du den Kuchen gegessen hast.*

The long sentence follows the same rule. German can add descriptions, time, place, and even another clause before it reaches the verb. The structure remains intact, but the meaning may remain unresolved until the final words.

Mark Twain filed this complaint before the rest of us could get to it:

> Whenever the literary German dives into a sentence, that is the last you are going to see of him till he emerges on the other side of his Atlantic with his verb in his mouth.

He had a point. Interrupting a German subordinate clause before the verb arrives can be difficult. You may know who or what the speaker is talking about, but you may not yet know what happened.

Simultaneous interpreters face the same problem at speed. When they interpret German into another language, they sometimes have to anticipate the verb before they have heard it.
