---
title: German Describes Things Instead of Naming Them
date: 2026-07-19
level: A2
description: German does not name things. It describes them, once, around 1750, and moves on.
---

German compound nouns join simple words together to describe a thing. This is a system with the confidence of a government office: why invent a new name when the old names can be bolted together?

- *die Handschuhe* — hand shoes (gloves)
- *der Kühlschrank* — cold cupboard (refrigerator)
- *die Glühbirne* — glow pear (lightbulb)
- *der Staubsauger* — dust sucker (vacuum cleaner)
- *das Faultier* — lazy animal (sloth)
- *die Nacktschnecke* — naked snail (slug)

The sloth is the clearest complaint I can file. English named the animal after one of the seven deadly sins. German looked at it, noted its behaviour, and called it a lazy animal. The German language may be severe, but it does at least tell you what it has noticed.

The same habit appears in words ending in *-zeug*, meaning stuff:

- *das Flugzeug* — fly-stuff (airplane)
- *das Spielzeug* — play-stuff (toy)
- *das Feuerzeug* — fire-stuff (lighter)

This made one sentence unusually easy for me to read:

*Mein Feuerzeug ist im Handschuh.*

My fire-stuff is in my hand-shoe.

I understood it on the first read. English, meanwhile, has decided that *flammable* and *inflammable* should mean the same thing, which is the sort of arrangement that makes a person grateful for German hand shoes.
